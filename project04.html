Project 4: Top Spinner

Build a top — and spin it.

The brief was to build a top and spin it. The card framed it from two sides: as a physicist, which properties make a top spin longest and stay stable; as an engineer, how to raise its circular momentum and which gear ratios get it turning fastest. I had fifteen minutes and worked alone.

I went after speed. Using every gear size in the set, I built four stages into a compound train: 36 to 8, then 28 to 20, then 28 to 20, then 36 to 8 again. Each stage's small gear shares an axle with the next stage's large one, so the ratios multiply rather than just add:

9/2 × 7/5 × 7/5 × 9/2 = 3969/100 — the top turns just under forty times for every single turn of the motor. A large motor drives the first stage.

The top itself is plain: a single beam with a blue wheel on the underside. That was a trade I made on purpose. With fifteen minutes I spent them on the gear train, because what I wanted to find out was how fast I could get the thing turning. The other half of the card — the physicist's half, what height and weight distribution make a top spin longest and stay stable — I did not get to.

It ran for about a minute and a half. I release it by hand: the card's extra challenge was to have the machine let go of the top by itself after spinning it, and I did not build that.
